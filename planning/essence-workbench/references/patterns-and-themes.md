# Patterns and themes

This essence surfaces repeated ideas, motifs, or structural tendencies across material. Use it when the goal is synthesis across many examples rather than extraction from one passage.

## Use when

- The value comes from cross-source synthesis, repetition, or recurring structure.
- You need to say what keeps showing up, what that pattern means, and where it breaks.

## Do not use when

- The reader mainly needs a faithful compression of one source rather than synthesis across many examples.
- The source is better modeled as categories, claims, or a chronology instead of recurring themes.

## Downstream intent note

- If the user already knows a likely later use, let that guide what details must survive, not how the material should be presented.
- Preserve representative examples, outliers, and why the pattern matters, but do not add narrative framing, section pacing, or final thematic layout here.

## Best editable shape

Prefer Markdown.

```markdown
# <theme set>

## Theme: <name>
- Description: <what repeats>
- Evidence:
  - <example>
- Why it matters: <interpretation>

## Outliers
- <what does not fit the pattern>
```

## What good pattern/theme content does

- Finds recurring meaning across examples, not just repeated words.
- Helps a reader see what is typical, important, and surprising.
- Balances synthesis with traceability back to evidence.

## Common failure modes

- Treating one-off anecdotes as themes.
- Over-smoothing contradictions until the synthesis feels fake.
- Listing patterns without saying why they matter.

## Preserve from the source

- Representative examples, spread across sources, and context.
- Outliers or negative cases that challenge the theme.
- The reason the pattern matters for the larger question.

## Pre-save checks

- Each theme is supported by multiple concrete examples.
- Contradictions and edge cases are still visible.
- The set feels saturated rather than repetitive.
