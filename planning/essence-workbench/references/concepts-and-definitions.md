# Concepts and definitions

This essence captures the important ideas in a source, what they mean, and how they connect. Use it when the goal is understanding a domain, model, or vocabulary.

## Use when

- The reader needs to understand terms, distinctions, and the logic of a domain.
- Definitions, non-examples, and relationships between ideas matter more than chronology or tasks.

## Do not use when

- The source is primarily a procedure, a set of actions, or a sequence of events.
- The main structure is stakeholder stance, option comparison, or argumentation rather than concept meaning.

## Downstream intent note

- If the user already knows a likely later use, let that guide what details must survive, not how the material should be presented.
- Preserve precise wording, nearby distinctions, and examples, but do not add lesson flow, card layout, or glossary page design here.

## Best editable shape

Prefer Markdown.

```markdown
# <domain>

## Core concepts
### <concept>
- Definition: <plain-language definition>
- Why it matters: <importance>
- Related concepts: <links>
- Example: <example>

## Open questions
- <gap>
```

## What good concepts content does

- Makes the domain easier to think with, not just easier to memorize.
- Defines each term by what it is and how it differs from nearby terms.
- Connects concepts into a system instead of a loose glossary.

## Common failure modes

- Circular definitions or paraphrases with no real clarification.
- Technical wording that stays opaque to the target reader.
- Terms listed in isolation with no relationships.

## Preserve from the source

- Precision where formal wording matters.
- Distinctions between adjacent or easily confused concepts.
- Examples or non-examples that sharpen understanding.

## Pre-save checks

- A reader can explain each concept back in plain language.
- Similar concepts are clearly separable.
- The set reveals the structure of the domain, not just vocabulary.
