# Arguments and counterarguments

This essence captures a position, the case for it, and the strongest objections against it. Use it when the source is adversarial, persuasive, or contested.

## Use when

- The material is organized around competing positions, objections, and rebuttals.
- Fairly representing disagreement is more important than compressing everything into one neutral statement.

## Do not use when

- You mainly need an evidence audit for discrete claims rather than a live clash of positions.
- A side-by-side option comparison would be cleaner than a debate structure.

## Downstream intent note

- If the user already knows a likely later use, let that guide what details must survive, not how the material should be presented.
- Preserve live tensions, assumptions, and unresolved weak spots, but do not script debate pacing, speakers, or rhetorical flourishes here.

## Best editable shape

Prefer Markdown.

```markdown
# <position>

## Main argument
- <claim>
- <support>

## Counterarguments
- <objection>

## Rebuttals
- <response>

## Open weaknesses
- <remaining vulnerability>
```

## What good argument content does

- Represents each side strongly enough that its supporters would recognize it.
- Keeps the live points of disagreement visible.
- Shows how objections and rebuttals actually connect.

## Common failure modes

- Straw-manning one side or collapsing nuance into pro/con slogans.
- Mixing factual disputes with value disputes.
- Hiding unresolved weaknesses.

## Preserve from the source

- Claim boundaries, assumptions, evidence strength, and conditions.
- Genuine objections, not simplified caricatures.
- Remaining vulnerabilities even after rebuttal.

## Pre-save checks

- A proponent of either side would call the summary fair.
- Counterevidence is represented honestly.
- Assumptions and unresolved weak spots are explicit.
