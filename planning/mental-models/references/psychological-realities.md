# Psychological Realities Library

How humans actually work — not errors to correct, but features to understand and work with.

---

## Motivation & Drive

| Model | What It Is | When to Use |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Motivation** | Internal drive (curiosity, mastery) vs external reward (money, praise). Extrinsic rewards can crowd out intrinsic motivation. | Management, education, self-motivation. Be careful: paying people for what they love can make them love it less. |
| **Identity-Based Behavior** | People act to be consistent with who they think they are. "I am a person who..." is more powerful than "I should..." | Habit formation ("I'm a runner" vs "I should run"), persuasion, culture change. Frame desired behavior as identity. |
| **Flow State** | Optimal performance happens when challenge slightly exceeds skill. Too easy = boredom. Too hard = anxiety. | Work design, learning, practice. Match difficulty to ability. Remove distractions. Clear goals. Immediate feedback. |
| **Ego Depletion / Cognitive Load** | Willpower and decision-making draw from a finite pool. Tired = worse decisions. | Scheduling (do hard things first), habit design (automate decisions), understanding why you binge eat at night or why judges give harsher sentences before lunch. |
| **Somatic Markers (Damasio)** | You decide emotionally first ("gut feeling"), then rationalize logically. Your body often knows before your mind. | When logic isn't working — check what your body feels. Important decisions often have a physical component you're ignoring. |

## Happiness & Satisfaction

| Model | What It Is | When to Use |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Hedonic Adaptation** | We return to baseline happiness after positive or negative events. The raise, the new car, the promotion — they feel great for a while, then become the new normal. | Life planning, purchases, "will this actually make me happier?" Experiences tend to produce more lasting happiness than possessions. |
| **Impact Bias** | We overestimate how long and how intensely future events will affect our emotions. Both good and bad. | "This will ruin me" — probably not. "This will make me forever happy" — also probably not. |
| **Hedonic Treadmill** | The cycle of wanting, getting, adapting, wanting more. Constant pursuit without lasting satisfaction. | Recognizing when you're on the treadmill. Shifting from acquisition to appreciation. |

## Belief & Dissonance

| Model | What It Is | When to Use |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Cognitive Dissonance** | Holding two conflicting beliefs creates psychological discomfort. We resolve it by changing a belief, adding a new belief, or reducing the importance of the conflict. | Understanding why smokers rationalize ("my grandfather smoked and lived to 90"). Why people double down on bad investments. Why cult members believe harder after failed prophecies. |
| **Belief Perseverance** | Once a belief is formed, it's remarkably resistant to contradictory evidence. Even when the original evidence is debunked. | Why first impressions stick. Why correcting misinformation often fails. You need to replace a belief, not just remove it. |
| **Effort Justification** | We attribute greater value to outcomes we had to work hard for. The IKEA effect — we value furniture we assembled ourselves. | Understanding why people defend bad processes they helped create. Why expensive = better (even when it's not). |

## Self & Perception

| Model | What It Is | When to Use |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Spotlight Effect** | We overestimate how much others notice our appearance, behavior, and mistakes. | Public speaking, social anxiety, embarrassment. Nobody noticed your small mistake. They're too busy worrying about their own. |
| **Impostor Syndrome** | Feeling like a fraud despite evidence of competence. Attributing success to luck, timing, or欺骗. | Common in high-achievers, people entering new fields, minorities in homogeneous environments. The feeling doesn't mean you're a fraud — it means you're growing. |
| **Self-Serving Bias** | We take credit for successes and blame failures on circumstances. "I succeeded because I'm skilled; I failed because of bad luck." | Self-reflection: flip it. "What did I do to cause this failure? What external factors helped my success?" |
| **Better-Than-Average Effect** | Most people rate themselves as above average on positive traits — which is statistically impossible. | Calibration check: if you think you're above average, ask "at what specifically?" Vague self-assessment is usually wrong. |

## Decision & Behavior

| Model | What It Is | When to Use |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Loss Aversion** | Losses hurt approximately twice as much as equivalent gains feel good. We're more motivated to avoid losing $100 than to gain $100. | Framing decisions, negotiation, change management. Frame changes as avoiding losses rather than gaining benefits. |
| **Status Quo Bias** | We prefer things to stay the same, even when change would be beneficial. The default option has enormous power. | Understanding inertia, designing defaults (opt-out vs opt-in), recognizing when "but we've always done it this way" is bias, not wisdom. |
| **Present Bias** | We systematically overvalue immediate rewards vs future ones. "I'll start tomorrow" is the voice of present bias. | Habits, saving, health. Use commitment devices (lock away the零食, auto-transfer to savings). Make the future self's preference the default. |
| **Hyperbolic Discounting** | The value we place on rewards drops steeply for near-future, then flattens. We'd rather have $50 today than $100 in a year, but we'd rather have $100 in 5 years than $50 in 4 years. | Understanding procrastination, addiction, saving behavior. The discount curve isn't rational — it's psychological. |

## Social Psychology

| Model | What It Is | When to Use |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Fundamental Attribution Error** | We explain others' behavior by their personality ("they're lazy") but our own behavior by circumstances ("I was stuck in traffic"). | Interpersonal conflict: assume situational causes first. "They're not ignoring me — they're probably overwhelmed." |
| **Actor-Observer Bias** | Related to above: actors (us) see our behavior as situationally caused; observers see actors' behavior as dispositionally caused. | When someone judges you harshly, remember: they can't see your circumstances. When you judge others, remember: you can't see theirs. |
| **Bystander Effect** | The more people present during an emergency, the less likely any one person is to help. Diffusion of responsibility. | If you need help, point at a specific person: "You, in the red shirt, call 911." Don't say "someone help." |
| **Mere Exposure Effect** | We develop a preference for things simply because we're familiar with them. Familiarity breeds liking, not contempt. | Marketing (repeated exposure), relationships (proximity breeds友谊), understanding why you like the music you grew up with. |

## How to Use This Library

These models aren't about spotting errors — they're about understanding the operating system:

1. **When someone's behavior seems irrational** → Check if it's actually a predictable psychological pattern
2. **When you're designing for humans** → Account for these realities, don't fight them
3. **When you're frustrated with yourself** → You're not broken, you're human. These are features, not bugs.
4. **When you want to change behavior** → Work with these patterns, not against them

**Key insight:** Biases are psychological realities viewed as errors. But they're not errors — they're heuristics that evolved for good reasons. The goal isn't to eliminate them (impossible) but to know when they help and when they hurt.
