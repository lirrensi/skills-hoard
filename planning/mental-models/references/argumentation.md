# Argumentation & Evidence Quality

How to build and evaluate arguments well — not just spot bad ones.

---

## Core Concepts

| Concept | What It Is | How to Apply |
|---------|-----------|--------------|
| **Claim / Evidence / Reasoning** | Every argument has three parts: what you assert, what supports it, and why the support matters | Structure your own arguments this way. Check others' arguments for missing parts. |
| **Burden of Proof** | The person making the claim must provide evidence. Not the skeptic. | "Prove it" is a valid response to unsupported claims. |
| **Falsifiability** | A claim that can't be proven wrong isn't useful. What would change your mind? | If nothing could disprove it, it's not making a real claim. |
| **Steelmanning** | Construct the strongest possible version of your opponent's argument before arguing against it | Do this before every debate. If you can't steelman, you don't understand their position. |
| **Converging Evidence** | Multiple weak signals from different sources can add up to strong evidence | Don't dismiss a line of evidence just because it's weak alone. Look for convergence. |
| **Extraordinary Claims** | Claims that violate well-established knowledge require proportionally stronger evidence | Sagan Standard: "Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence." |

---

## Evidence Hierarchies

Not all evidence is equal. From weakest to strongest:

| Level | Type | Strength |
|-------|------|----------|
| 1 | **Anecdote** — personal story | Weakest. Compelling but unreliable. |
| 2 | **Expert opinion** — authority says so | Better, but experts disagree and are biased. |
| 3 | **Observational study** — correlation in data | Shows association, not causation. Confounders lurk. |
| 4 | **Controlled experiment** — randomized, controlled | Much stronger. Isolates causal effects. |
| 5 | **Systematic review / Meta-analysis** — many studies combined | Strongest. Reduces individual study biases. |

**Key rules:**
- Anecdotes are not data
- One study is not a conclusion
- Correlation is not causation (but it's a hint worth investigating)
- The plural of anecdote is not data — unless you systematically collect them

## Evaluating Arguments

### Checklist for any argument:

1. **Is the claim clear?** Vague claims can't be evaluated.
2. **Is the evidence relevant?** Does it actually support the claim?
3. **Is the evidence sufficient?** One data point isn't enough.
4. **Is the evidence reliable?** Who produced it? What's their incentive?
5. **Are there alternative explanations?** Could the evidence support a different conclusion?
6. **Is the reasoning valid?** Does the logic follow, or is there a fallacy?
7. **What's missing?** What evidence would the arguer prefer you not see?

### Red flags in arguments:

- Attacking the person instead of the argument (ad hominem)
- Using emotion instead of evidence (appeal to emotion)
- Citing authority outside their domain (misplaced authority)
- Cherry-picking data that supports the conclusion
- Ignoring the strongest counter-arguments
- Using jargon to obscure rather than clarify
- Moving the goalposts when challenged

## Constructive Argumentation

### How to argue well:

1. **State your claim clearly** — one sentence, no hedging
2. **Lead with your strongest evidence** — not your weakest
3. **Acknowledge counter-evidence** — address it directly
4. **Explain your reasoning** — don't just list facts
5. **Specify what would change your mind** — show intellectual honesty
6. **Distinguish certainty levels** — "I'm 90% sure" vs "I know for certain"

### How to disagree productively:

| Level | What You Do |
|-------|-------------|
| **Level 1** | Reframe their position back to them — "So you're saying..." |
| **Level 2** | Identify where you agree — "I agree that X, but..." |
| **Level 3** | Steelman their argument — make it stronger than they did |
| **Level 4** | State your disagreement with specific evidence |
| **Level 5** | Propose a test or criteria that could resolve the disagreement |

## Common Evidence Mistakes

| Mistake | What Goes Wrong |
|---------|----------------|
| **Cherry-picking** | Selecting only evidence that supports your conclusion |
| **Moving the goalposts** | Changing the criteria when your claim is challenged |
| **Gish gallop** | Overwhelming with many weak arguments instead of one strong one |
| **Argument from ignorance** | "You can't prove it's false, so it's true" |
| **False balance** | Giving equal weight to a strong position and a fringe position |
| **Anecdotal evidence** | Using stories instead of systematic data |
| **Survivorship bias in evidence** | Only looking at successes, ignoring failures |
