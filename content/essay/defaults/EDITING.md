# Editing Principles Reference Template

This file is the editorial brain of the essay skill.

The orchestrator and step files should not reinvent these principles in scattered little versions. They should read this file, use it consistently, and apply it according to the user's goal.

The preferred setup is to place an editable `EDITING.md` in the **project root** and let that file govern the folder. This bundled file should be treated as a template/reference that can be copied into the root when needed.

If `VOICE.md` exists in the project's root folder, use it alongside the root `EDITING.md`. `EDITING.md` governs craft and editorial judgment. `VOICE.md` governs how the piece should sound.

---

## Core editorial stance

- Edit like a professional editor, not a content spinner.
- Improve structure, clarity, pacing, and force without flattening the author's personality.
- Prefer the smallest effective intervention.
- Preserve what is alive in the draft.
- Cut what is baggy, repetitive, evasive, or unearned.
- The goal is not to make the essay sound smarter. The goal is to make it work.
- The default target is broad-reader clarity: a bright 12-year-old should follow most of it, and an 18-year-old should be able to feel moved, provoked, or energized by it.

## Non-negotiable principles

These principles were already scattered across the old step files. They now live here.

### 1. Prefer synthesis over replacement
If the user already has good material, unify and strengthen it instead of casually rewriting everything from scratch.

### 2. Don't over-polish
Not every sentence needs to be dazzling. Some sentences just need to do their job cleanly.

### 3. Avoid mechanical transitions
Do not lean on dead connective tissue like:
- Furthermore
- Additionally
- In conclusion
- On the other hand

Transitions should feel like thought moving forward, not like presentation software.

### 4. Earn the ending
An ending should feel inevitable in hindsight, not inflated on arrival. If the essay reaches for profundity it has not earned, pull it back.

### 5. Preserve register
If the draft is casual, witty, internet-native, blunt, warm, or Reddit-like, keep that unless the user asks otherwise.

### 6. Don't upscale the audience by accident
Default to intelligent broad readers. Do not quietly rewrite the piece for specialists, academics, or prestige-magazine imaginary judges unless the user clearly wants that.

### 7. Specificity beats vagueness
Concrete examples, observed details, and named tensions almost always beat abstract summary.

### 8. Clarity beats inflation
Do not make language fancier just to make it feel more serious. Sharper usually beats grander.

### 8a. Name the thing plainly
If the draft is circling the point with hedging, abstraction, or polite fog, say the thing directly.

### 8b. Strip sentences to clean essentials
Cut words, clauses, and passive constructions that do not carry real meaning. If a sentence works better in a simpler form, use the simpler form.

### 9. Diagnose before decorating
If the argument, structure, or pacing is broken, surface that. Line polish is not a substitute for an essay that actually works.

### 10. Keep the best lines
If the author has a line with real personality or force, protect it where possible.

## Essay craft knowledge

## Publication-inspired broad-reader model

Use this as a practical style target for accessible, high-quality essays:

- **Longreads**: human stakes, room to breathe, narrative pull
- **The Atlantic**: public-facing clarity, strong framing, clear relevance
- **The New Yorker**: precision, control, observation, polish without strain
- **Guernica**: moral seriousness, emotional consequence, humane weight
- **New York Times Magazine / Opinion**: readability for large audiences, direct setup, fast legibility
- **The Point**: reflective intelligence without jargon, thought that still feels human

Combined effect:
- clear enough for a wide audience
- serious without sounding superior
- emotionally alive without melodrama
- thoughtful without academic scaffolding
- conversational without becoming sloppy

If a passage sounds like it is trying to impress rather than connect, pull it back.

---

## Situation versus story

Many drafts know the **situation** before they know the **story**.

- **Situation** = what happened
- **Story** = what the writer discovered, lost, misread, resisted, or finally understood

If a draft can only summarize events, it may still be pre-essay material.

### Editorial test for story
Ask:
- What is this piece really about beneath the plot?
- What changed in the writer's understanding?
- What tension, contradiction, desire, fear, or loss gives the events meaning?

If the answer is still just a recap, the essay likely needs more reflection, sharper stakes, or a clearer emotional center.

---

## Universal through the particular

Broad emotional reach usually comes from precise material, not broad declarations.

- specific scenes beat generic summary
- concrete detail beats abstract emotion words
- one lived moment can carry a larger truth better than a paragraph of explanation

When the essay tries to sound universal too early, it often becomes vague. Let the larger meaning emerge from the particulars.

Do not trade specificity for simplicity. Precise examples often make the writing easier to understand.

---

## Emotional truth without overexplanation

The goal is not melodrama, confession for its own sake, or therapy-speak. The goal is emotional honesty that serves the reader.

Good emotional handling often means:
- admitting the uncomfortable thing
- naming the real tension instead of the polite version
- showing the cost, not just the concept
- trusting the reader to feel what the scene already carries

Watch for places where the draft explains the feeling after the feeling has already landed.

Cut or reduce lines that do things like:
- tell the reader exactly what to feel
- summarize the emotion a second time
- add moral commentary that weakens the scene
- flatten ambiguity that should stay alive

---

## Scene, summary, and reflection

Strong essays breathe by moving between:

- **scene** — concrete action, dialogue, gesture, sensory detail, real-time texture
- **summary** — efficient movement through time or background
- **reflection** — what the writer now sees, questions, or understands

Too much summary makes the draft feel distant.
Too much scene can feel shapeless if nothing is being made of it.
Too much reflection can feel lecture-like.

### Editorial test for pacing
Ask:
- Where should the essay slow down and become a scene?
- Where should it compress instead of narrating every step?
- Where should reflection arrive so the meaning feels earned rather than stapled on?

---

## Specificity, embodiment, and sensory proof

If the draft uses abstract feeling words, look for the physical evidence.

Instead of relying only on lines like:
- "I was nervous"
- "it was heartbreaking"
- "she was angry"

look for details like:
- gestures
- bodily reactions
- objects handled a certain way
- textures, sounds, smells, spatial tension

The aim is not decorative description. The aim is evidence the reader can feel.

Sensory detail also builds trust. Readers believe a scene more readily when it feels observed rather than vaguely announced.

---

## Accessibility and plain-language pressure

This skill should default to broad-reader clarity.

That means:
- plain language over prestige phrasing
- direct syntax over clause-stacking
- strong verbs over padded noun phrases
- clear referents over fuzzy "this" or "it"
- live sentences over academic scaffolding

Watch for academic or inflated habits such as:
- "it is important to note that"
- "one might argue"
- "in conclusion"
- passive constructions that hide agency
- abstract nouns doing the work of verbs

The goal is not to dumb the piece down. The goal is to remove friction between the draft and the reader.

### Fast accessibility test
Ask:
- Could a bright 12-year-old follow the literal meaning of most sentences?
- Could an 18-year-old feel the force, stakes, or emotional pressure without needing a glossary?
- Does this sound like a smart person talking clearly, or like a nervous person dressing up their ideas?

If the sentence fails that test, simplify it.

## Conversational authority

The voice should feel like a real person speaking clearly to another real person.

That does **not** mean casual in a lazy way. It means:
- no fake intellectual posturing
- no needless jargon
- no stiff school-essay framing
- no "as we can see" professor throat-clearing
- no pretending uncertainty is depth

Good conversational authority sounds:
- direct
- calm or charged on purpose
- specific
- readable aloud
- willing to make a point plainly

The essay can be angry, inspired, urgent, tender, or cutting. It just should not be foggy.

---

## Moral and emotional charge

For essays meant to move general readers, pressure matters.

The piece should not only communicate information. It should create some felt consequence:
- urgency
- recognition
- indignation
- tenderness
- unease
- resolve

This usually comes from naming stakes clearly, choosing vivid specifics, and refusing euphemism where the truth is sharper.

Do not drain the essay's heat in the name of polish.

---

## Ladder of abstraction

Essays often need to move up and down levels cleanly:

- concrete example
- pattern or tension
- implication or insight

If the draft stays only concrete, it may feel vivid but slight.
If it stays only abstract, it may feel intelligent but dead.

Good essays alternate between the seen thing and the meaning of the thing.

---

## Thought arc

An essay should not feel like a pile of anecdotes or a timeline with commentary attached.

It should have a **thought arc**:
- a question sharpening
- a tension deepening
- a pattern becoming visible
- an understanding changing shape

Each section should move the reader's understanding, not just add more material.

## Openings: what actually hooks

An opening works when it creates forward pressure. That usually comes from one or more of these:

- a sharp observation
- a cleanly stated tension
- a surprising contrast
- a specific concrete example
- a question the reader genuinely wants answered
- a line that establishes voice immediately

An opening fails when it does any of the following:

- throat-clears for too long
- announces the topic without creating stakes
- starts too abstract
- sounds like a school essay introducing itself
- promises a level of seriousness or insight the body never cashes

### Editorial test for openings
Ask:
- Why should the reader keep going after paragraph one?
- What tension or curiosity has been created?
- Does this sound like the essay that follows, or like a fake formal wrapper?

---

## The spine: thesis, throughline, one big idea

Every essay needs a spine: the single thing it is really trying to say.

This does not have to be an academic thesis sentence. It can be:
- a claim
- a tension
- a reframing
- an insight
- a problem with an angle

But the piece needs one central thread. If the draft contains five interesting thoughts and no governing center, the reader feels drift.

### Editorial test for the spine
Ask:
- What is the one big idea here?
- Can the essay be summarized in one honest sentence?
- Does each major section feed that sentence, complicate it, or earn it?

If not, the piece may contain good material without having a shape yet.

---

## Structure: clusters, sequence, and flow

Most messy essays improve when raw material is grouped into natural clusters, then ordered so each section makes the next one legible.

Good structure usually does at least one of these:
- escalates tension
- deepens the claim
- moves from observation to implication
- moves from problem to consequence to meaning
- contrasts what seems true with what is actually true

Bad structure often feels like:
- repeated points in slightly different wording
- a smart digression that steals the center
- good paragraphs in the wrong order
- a section that arrives before the reader has the context to absorb it

### Editorial test for structure
Ask:
- Why is this section here?
- Why is it here and not earlier or later?
- What changes in the reader's understanding after this section?

If the answer is "not much," the section may be dead weight.

---

## Tension: how essays build and pay off

An essay needs movement. Not plot, exactly, but felt motion.

Tension can come from:
- an unresolved question
- a contradiction
- an uncomfortable truth
- a gap between what people say and what they do
- pressure between two plausible positions
- a pattern the essay keeps clarifying

Tension pays off when the essay:
- answers the question honestly
- resolves or sharpens the contradiction
- reframes the issue in a satisfying way
- lands on a conclusion that feels earned by the path taken

Tension dies when:
- the essay explains everything too early
- sections repeat rather than develop
- the strongest point arrives too soon and nothing surpasses it
- the ending merely restates rather than transforms

---

## Dead zones: what they feel like and how to fix them

A dead zone is a stretch where the essay loses heat.

It often feels like:
- the same point being made again with less force
- summary replacing movement
- abstraction replacing example
- background material with no clear function
- a digression that does not earn its space

### Ways to fix a dead zone
- cut it entirely
- merge it into the surrounding section
- add a concrete example
- sharpen the claim it is trying to support
- move it somewhere else
- turn explanation into tension

Editorial question: if this paragraph vanished, would the essay weaken, or just get shorter?

---

## Made versus earned

One of the most important distinctions in essay writing:

- A point is **made** when it is stated.
- A point is **earned** when the reader feels the essay has carried them to it.

Points are earned through:
- concrete evidence
- progression
- contrast
- implication
- rhythm and emphasis
- examples that do real work

A point is not earned just because it is true, fashionable, or phrased confidently.

### Editorial test
Ask:
- Has the essay shown enough for this line to land?
- Would a skeptical reader accept that this follows?
- Is this conclusion doing more work than the body has set up?

---

## Paragraph craft

Good paragraphs usually have:
- a clear job
- a focal move
- enough specificity to feel grounded
- an ending that creates momentum or resonance

Weak paragraphs often:
- start late
- repeat themselves
- contain multiple unrelated jobs
- explain the point before making it vivid
- drift upward into abstraction before the reader has anything to picture

When revising paragraphs:
- find the real center
- cut the warm-up
- keep one dominant move
- end on the strongest useful line
- check whether the paragraph needs more scene, more summary, or more reflection

---

## Transitions that don't sound mechanical

Good transitions usually do one of these:
- extend the previous point
- complicate it
- turn it
- narrow from general to specific
- widen from example to implication

Useful transition patterns:
- "That matters because..."
- "But the real problem is..."
- "Which sounds reasonable until..."
- "The deeper issue is..."
- "This is where..."

The transition should carry pressure, not just sequence.

---

## Intros and conclusions

### Intros
The intro should usually:
- establish voice
- create curiosity or stakes
- orient the reader just enough
- point toward the spine

It does **not** need to summarize the whole essay like a school assignment.

### Conclusions
The conclusion should:
- land the essay's actual insight
- give the reader a sense of arrival
- echo or transform an earlier tension
- stop at the right moment

It should not:
- suddenly widen into grand universal claims
- add a new major argument
- over-explain the meaning
- perform profundity instead of delivering it
- flatten emotional complexity into a school-style moral

When possible, let the conclusion resonate through image, pressure, or a changed understanding rather than a school-style recap.

---

## Line-level editing moves that usually help

At the sentence level, common upgrades include:

- cut filler such as `very`, `really`, `actually`, `basically`, `somewhat`, `quite`
- replace weak verbs with more specific ones
- cut duplicate ideas stated twice in different clothes
- break overloaded sentences when clarity drops
- keep sentence variety so the prose has rhythm
- replace vague abstractions with observable detail when the draft needs life
- remove unnecessary adverbs that tell the reader how to feel

If a sentence sounds impressive but not human, simplify it.

---

## Why Reddit-style writing works when it works

Casual broad-audience writing can be excellent when it has:

- specificity
- directness
- clear stakes
- low ego
- little or no throat-clearing
- sentences that sound like a person thinking clearly in public
- confidence without false grandeur

It fails when it becomes:
- smug
- too inside-baseball
- shapeless ranting
- repetition disguised as emphasis
- ironic at the expense of meaning

The goal is not to sanitize this voice. The goal is to keep its energy while giving it form.

---

## Editing priorities by mode

### Finalize
Prioritize:
- finding the spine
- grouping and ordering material
- turning clusters into readable sections
- making the piece feel like one essay instead of a pile

### Review
Prioritize:
- argument integrity
- structure and pacing
- dead zones
- whether claims are earned
- whether the piece is actually close to done or only feels close

### Polish
Prioritize:
- rhythm
- clarity
- repeated words and filler
- awkward sentences
- consistency

Do not use polish to hide structural weakness.

---

## What to say when something isn't working

Be direct, not vague.

Good:
- "This section repeats the previous point without adding pressure."
- "The ending reaches farther than the essay earns."
- "The opening is clear but not yet compelling."
- "This paragraph has useful material, but it currently has no single job."

Bad:
- "This is weak."
- "Needs work."
- "Could be smoother."

Name the problem in craft terms the user can actually use.
