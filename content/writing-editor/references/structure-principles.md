# Structure Principles

These principles distill the strongest recurring ideas from the editorial source material in `IDEAS/`. They explain what strong structural writing is doing underneath the surface.

## 1. Fix the frame before the paint

Structural problems outrank stylistic problems. If the spine is broken, sentence polish is mostly wasted effort.

- Concept and structure set the terms for everything that follows.
- A clean sentence cannot rescue a confused sequence.
- When in doubt, diagnose the frame before commenting on finesse.

## 2. A piece needs one dominant center of gravity

Many weak drafts are not empty. They are crowded. They try to be about several things at once.

- Find the controlling question, promise, or claim.
- Ask whether most of the draft truly serves that center.
- Tangents can be strong writing and still be structurally wrong.

## 3. The opening creates a contract

The opening tells the reader what kind of journey this is and what payoff to expect.

- A strong opening sets direction, not just mood.
- Every promise creates debt.
- If the body fails to deliver what the opening implies, the piece feels dishonest or unfinished.

## 4. Order is part of meaning

Good structure is not just having all the right parts. It is having them in the right sequence.

- Readers need setup before payoff.
- Readers need context at the moment of need, not in a dump too early or too late.
- If the reader asks, "Why am I hearing this now?" the sequence is doing extra damage even when each paragraph is individually good.

## 5. Setup and payoff govern trust

When a draft introduces a thread, distinction, example, question, or tension, the reader assumes it matters.

- If a setup never pays off, the draft leaks trust.
- If a payoff arrives without preparation, it feels unearned.
- Threads should either close, transform, or be intentionally cut.

## 6. Coherence is not the same as correctness

This skill checks whether the piece agrees with itself and supports its own promises.

- Do not drift into arguing whether the author's worldview is right.
- Do flag contradictions, unsupported leaps, and internal reversals.
- A draft can be coherent and wrong. It can also be true and badly structured.

## 7. Pacing is about density, not just length

Something can be short and still feel slow. Something can be long and still feel easy to move through.

- Dense material often needs more framing, spacing, or slower explanation.
- Light material may need compression.
- Pace problems often come from information load, not word count alone.

## 8. Endings must close the loop

The ending should feel like the necessary landing point of the draft, not a final paragraph attached at the end.

- It should close what the opening opened.
- It should not claim more than the body earned.
- A strong ending changes the reader's sense of what the piece has accomplished.

## 9. Structure review is guidance, not takeover

The editor's job is to point at the weak point and explain its effect.

- Diagnose the break.
- Explain why it matters.
- Suggest the kind of structural operation needed: cut, merge, reorder, define earlier, pay off later, narrow the spine.
- Use the cut test when helpful: if removing a section does not weaken what follows, that section may not be load-bearing.
- Do not seize authorship of the draft.

## 10. What to watch for repeatedly

- competing spines
- false setup
- missing bridge
- term drift
- redundant section
- non-load-bearing section
- unearned ending
- thread opened but never closed
- section that should be appendix, background, or cut text instead of body material
