# Style Principles

These principles distill the strongest recurring ideas from the editorial source material in `IDEAS/`. They explain what strong style and readability are doing underneath the surface.

## 1. Style is reader experience, not ornament

The point of style is not to sound impressive. It is to help the writing land cleanly and with force.

- Good style reduces friction without killing personality.
- A stylish line that slows comprehension is often a net loss.
- The test is not "Do I like this sentence?" but "What does this sentence do to the reader?"

## 2. Failure is functional, not personal

Do not treat a passage as failing merely because it is not your preferred style. Treat it as failing when it breaks the operating principles of readable, trustworthy prose.

- A passage fails when it interferes with comprehension, pacing, emphasis, audience fit, tonal coherence, or reader trust.
- Name the actual failure mode: ambiguity, density, rhythm drag, weak bridge, jargon fog, over-explanation, under-explanation, or consistency leak.
- Judge the writing by what it does, not by whether it matches a private preference.

## 3. Clarity usually reflects clarity of thought

Muddy prose often signals muddy thinking.

- When a sentence keeps collapsing under revision, the underlying idea may be under-formed.
- Vagueness is sometimes a conceptual problem disguised as a language problem.
- Naming the actual source of confusion helps the writer revise more intelligently.

## 4. Tone is a relationship

Tone is not just formal versus casual. It expresses attitude toward the subject, the reader, and the self.

- Toward the subject: avoid worship, disdain, and theatrical certainty.
- Toward the reader: avoid condescension, coyness, and needless aggression.
- Toward the self: avoid false modesty, defensiveness, and prestige performance.

## 5. Rhythm carries meaning

Sentence length, paragraph size, cadence, and pause structure affect how ideas land.

- Monotony numbs the reader.
- Over-compression makes dense material feel punishing.
- Sometimes expansion improves pace because it gives heavy material enough air.

## 6. Attention is evidence

When the prose causes attention to slip, that is diagnostic information, not just a mood.

- Read-aloud testing often reveals what silent reading forgives.
- If the ear trips, the sentence may be acoustically wrong even when it is grammatically legal.
- Attention usually drops for reasons: repetition, decorative flourish, weak progression, shapeless density, or dead rhythm.

## 7. Orientation lowers cognitive load

Readers need a yardstick: what this is, where they are, and why the next part matters.

- Topic sentences and headings should orient, not merely label.
- Paragraph bridges are load-bearing.
- When links stay implicit, the reader does repair work that the prose should have done.

## 8. Specificity beats prestige fog

Abstractions, hedges, and inflated language often make prose feel smart while saying less.

- Prefer precise claims to vague grandeur.
- Use jargon only when it earns its place through precision.
- Make the level of specificity match the audience and purpose.

## 9. Form serves understanding

The right shape depends on the job the prose is trying to do.

- Cutting is not automatically improvement.
- Sometimes expansion, spacing, or a clearer bridge improves pace more than compression.
- Judge form by what it does for understanding, rhythm, emphasis, and force.

## 10. Consistency builds trust

Consistency is not cosmetic. It helps the reader stay oriented and believe the writer is in control.

- Stable terminology prevents needless re-parsing.
- Consistent labels, pronouns, and formatting reduce mental friction.
- Mechanical inconsistency can make even good ideas feel unstable.

## 11. Preserve voice without importing your own

The editor should strengthen the writer's voice, not replace it.

- Fix what causes confusion, drag, or mismatch.
- Keep what gives the prose life.
- Do not turn everything into the editor's preferred style.

## 12. Example rewrites are tools, not outcomes

Sometimes the fastest way to clarify a note is to show a local example.

- Keep examples short and clearly illustrative.
- Use them to show shape, not to take over the draft.
- The writer should leave with a better sense of what to do, not with a ghostwritten replacement.

## 13. What to watch for repeatedly

- sentence overload
- acoustic drag
- vague abstraction
- pronoun confusion
- jargon without payoff
- tone wobble
- paragraph bridge failure
- rhythm monotony
- uncashed claim
- weak support
- over-explaining to experts
- under-explaining to newcomers
- consistency failures that leak trust
