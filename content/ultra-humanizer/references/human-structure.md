# Human Structure: Paragraphs, Modules, Openings, Endings

> Stable craft reference. Paragraph and document moves that make prose navigable. Pairs with slop-shapes.md (what to remove) and human-reader.md (reader task targeting).

D1 constraint: every move below restructures feel and order only. Added specifics must come from the source text (reorder, unpack, or concretize what is stated). If a fact is not in the source, drop the claim or flag it to the user. Never invent.

## How to use this file

1. One paragraph gets one theme (entry 1).
2. Leads carry the benefit and takeaways add news (entry 2).
3. Commercial pages get scenario modules (entries 3, 4).
4. Identity lines stay plain, quirks stay on topic (entries 5, 6).
5. Endings follow the genre fork (entry 7). Openings skip the roadmap (entries 9, 10).

### 1. One Paragraph, One Theme

**Move:** Give each paragraph a single theme, stated in the first sentence. Any sentence on a different theme moves to its own paragraph or gets cut, no matter how much it cost to write.

**Do:** Read the first sentence of each paragraph in sequence. If the sequence tells a coherent story, the structure holds. If a sentence inside a paragraph belongs to another theme, evict it.

Before:

> Virtual reality is still a confusing product category. Few people can explain what urgent problem it solves. The latest headsets are still too expensive for mass buyers. So it will take time before we figure out why we need it.

After:

> Virtual reality is still a confusing product category. Few people can explain what urgent problem it solves. So it will take time before we figure out why we need it.
>
> Price is a separate question. The latest headsets are still too expensive for mass buyers. (Split into its own paragraph or cut if price is off topic.)

**Source:** Ilyakhov «Емкость абзаца» (chunk_05:639-667); backlog on item 21.

### 2. Lead States the Benefit, Takeaway Adds News

**Move:** Open explanatory paragraphs with the main point phrased as reader benefit, in plain words. Close with a rule, piece of advice, or generalization that ADDS information. Never repeat the lead in the last sentence. Domain gate: explanatory text only (manuals, how-tos, explainers). Opinion and essay paragraphs follow entry 7 instead.

**Do:** Rewrite the lead so a skimmer who reads only first sentences still gets the point. Then check the last sentence against the lead: if it restates, delete it or add news.

Before:

> Pick a deposit with capitalization. Then your income grows faster: each month the bank adds interest to the balance.

After:

> Income grows fastest in a deposit with capitalization. With capitalization the bank adds interest to the balance every month, so next month you earn interest on the interest. Open it before payday to catch the full cycle. (Lead carries the benefit; closer adds the timing rule.)

**Source:** Ilyakhov «Первое предложение абзаца», «Вывод к абзацу» (chunk_05:513-638); v2 answer-first instance.

### 3. Scenario Modules with Short Noun Labels

**Move:** On commercial, about, and service pages, organize by reader situation, not by your org chart. Each situation becomes a section titled with the reader task ("Working on a laptop", "Cakes needed tomorrow", "Delivery"). Inside the module: what you give, why you can, one example, one limit. Each module reads as a mini article.

**Do:** List the 3 to 6 situations the reader is actually in. Replace Overview/Features/Benefits headings with those situation labels. Cut modules with no real situation behind them.

Before:

> Our strengths. We offer competitive products and strong service. Our team is committed to quality across all dimensions.

After:

> Deadlines. Our specialty is cakes needed tomorrow. We bake through the night before the party, so they arrive fresh and on time. We cannot help only if the cake is needed today.
>
> Ingredients. No fondant: milk chocolate, jelly, natural caramel, glaze, flambe instead. One slice runs about the calories of a glass of sweet soda.

**Source:** Ilyakhov bakery and library modules (chunk_08:337-399); backlog item 23.

### 4. Module Craft: TLDR, Summary, Disclaimer, Reader Briefing

**Move:** D3 exception to the ban on roadmap scaffolding. Commercial AND literary texts may open a section with a short reader briefing: a one line TLDR, a two sentence summary, or a plain disclaimer of scope and limits. It helps the reader decide whether to read on. Keep it to one or two lines, in plain words, then start the substance.

**Do:** Write the briefing from stated section content only (D1). State who the section serves, what it covers, and what it skips. Never preview with cleverness or throat clearing.

Before:

> In this section we will explore various aspects of our delivery process and elucidate key considerations pertaining thereto.

After:

> TLDR: we deliver cakes ourselves across Moscow and Khimki in thermal bags, and we take responsibility for them arriving intact. Six tier showpieces we assemble on site.
>
> Disclaimer: this section covers delivery only. Timing is in the Deadlines section above.

**Source:** D3 decision (commercial plus literary modules); backlog item 23 conflict note.

### 5. Plain Identity Headline

**Move:** Open about pages and self descriptions with who you are plus what you are for, in a stranger plain words. Put the clever self label in sentence two at the earliest, never in the headline.

**Do:** Use words the reader already knows. Name the audience and the task. Cut metaphors ("engineer of joy", "orchestrating excellence") from line one.

Before:

> Orchestrating digital excellence for forward thinking brands.

After:

> I am Kai Aldridge. I build websites for small businesses.
>
> Shop owners hire me before a busy season to get online booking working. (Clever voice, if any, starts here.)

**Source:** Ilyakhov «Кто вы» (chunk_07:503-527); backlog item 24.

### 6. On Topic Quirk

**Move:** One odd, true, memorable detail is allowed AFTER the substance is solid, and only if it still carries a product fact. Garnish, never substitute. The skill older "weird detail" move lacked this constraint; this entry adds it.

**Do:** Draft the useful text first. Then add at most one quirk that proves a fact (easy cleanup, real tolerance, real origin). If the quirk proves nothing, cut it.

Before:

> We bake celebration cakes. Fun fact: I love horses.

After:

> We bake celebration cakes. We also keep a face cake: for pushing someone head first into cream. The water based mousse tastes good, rinses off, and does not stain clothes. (Quirk proves the mousse fact.)

**Source:** Ilyakhov «Изюминка» (chunk_07:849-862); backlog item 24b.

### 7. Ending Catalogue and the Genre Fork

**Move:** D4 fork. Essay, blog, and opinion texts end open: consequence, open question, provisional conclusion, or a detail that does not summarize. Commercial, about, and request texts end closed: the exact next act plus its constraint tied to a real deadline (see human-reader.md for the action brief).

**Do:** Pick one catalogue entry per genre. Essay side: consequence ("this means X happens next"), open question, provisional conclusion ("so far this suggests"), nonsummarizing detail. Commercial side: verb plus constraint plus deadline ("Call before Thursday and we bake it for Friday").

Before (essay, wrapped up):

> In conclusion, the tool is useful, reliable, and worth adopting.

After (essay, provisional plus consequence):

> The tool is useful. Whether it is worth adopting depends on whether your team will actually maintain it. So far the teams that assign one owner keep it; the rest let it rot.

Before (commercial, vague):

> Contact us to reach your goals.

After (commercial, act plus constraint plus deadline):

> Call before Thursday and we bake it for Friday. Need it today: that is the one thing we cannot do.

**Source:** v2 Closure (554-568) reworked by D4; backlog items 27, 32; Ilyakhov letter closings (chunk_08).

### 8. Kill the Outline Shape

**Move:** Port of v2 Structure-outline. Do not announce structure ("There are three reasons"). Let one point run longer than the others. Start in the middle, not with context. Come back to a dropped point later if needed.

**Do:** Delete roadmap sentences. Vary point lengths so one point clearly dominates. Open with the strongest reason, not the background.

Before:

> There are three reasons this works. First, it is fast. Second, it is reliable. Third, it is cheap.

After:

> It works because it is fast. Also reliable, which matters more than you would think. And cheap, though cheap is the part I keep worrying about.

**Source:** v2 § Structure-outline (482-496).

### 9. Answer First Opening

**Move:** Open with the reader task, the answer, or the hardest constraint. No chatbot framing, no "In this article we will explore". One instance lived buried in v2; this entry promotes it to a rule.

**Do:** Write the opening sentence so it works as a reply to the reader question. Delete warm up paragraphs. If backstory is needed, it goes in paragraph two.

Before:

> In this article, we will explore the benefits of automated testing.

After:

> I used to hate automated testing. Then a bug cost us a weekend. Here is the setup that would have caught it.

**Source:** v2 Imperfect Opening (598-612) plus item 7; Ilyakhov «Первое предложение абзаца» kicker (chunk_05:583-599).

### 10. Doc Level Rhythm (D6)

**Move:** Pace follows meaning, not a recipe. Slow down at the hard part (longer sentences, unpacked steps). Cut hard at the punchline (short sentences). Fragments only at peaks. Never alternate sentence lengths by counting words. That fixed cadence is itself detectable.

**Do:** Mark the hardest passage for the reader. Give it the most space and the plainest steps. Mark the one line the reader should remember. Give it the shortest sentence nearby. Read aloud: if the rhythm feels metronomic, redistribute length toward meaning.

Before (even tempo, recipe rhythm):

> The migration saves money. It reduces risk by a wide margin. It also improves morale across the team in a measurable way. Each benefit compounds over time in sequence.

After (slow at the hard part, cut at the punchline):

> The migration saves money, but the saving sits inside a tedious step nobody documents: every old report has to be repointed by hand, checked against last quarter, then signed off. Budget two afternoons for that. The payoff is simple. It sticks.

**Source:** D6 decision; replaces v2 Rhythm recipe (467-480).
