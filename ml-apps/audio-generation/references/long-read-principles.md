# Long Read Principles (Text → Audiobook)

Load this guide when preparing text for narration — the editorial principles for making eye-readable text work as ear-navigable audio. This is about *rewriting*, not generating. Read this before `long-reads.md` (which handles the technical pipeline).

---

## The Core Shift

Moving from **eye-readable** to **ear-navigable**. Eyes can scan, reread, and hold context visually. Ears are locked to linear time.

This is the same foundational shift as podcast — but simpler in one way and harder in another. Simpler because there's no dialogue to script, no two-host dynamic to manage. Harder because you lose the single most powerful engagement tool podcasts have: **the social dimension**. No banter, no reactions, no "wait, really?" moments. One voice, one stream, one chance to hold attention.

Everything below flows from this constraint.

---

## 1. The Redundancy Reversal

This is the principle that feels most wrong if you're trained in good writing.

In text, good adaptation means cutting redundancy. Tight, concise, no repetition.

In audio, you must **add** redundancy. Say the key point, then rephrase it, then reference it again later. Because listeners can't re-read a sentence.

**Text rule:** "Don't repeat yourself."
**Audio rule:** "Say it, say it differently, then remind them you said it."

This isn't a minor stylistic tweak. It fundamentally changes how you structure information density. What feels like padding in text is actually **navigation** in audio — each repetition is a re-anchoring point for a listener who may have drifted for a moment.

Practical techniques:
- **State, then rephrase:** "The key finding was X. Put another way..."
- **Callback references:** "Remember that statistic from earlier? Here's why it matters now..."
- **Summary beats:** After a dense section, compress it into one sentence before moving on
- **Transition recaps:** "So that's the problem. Now let's look at the solution."

---

## 2. The Temporal Prison

Text lets the reader control time. They skim, skip, slow down, re-read. Audio removes this entirely. The listener is locked into the narrator's pacing.

This creates constraints that don't exist in text:

- **You must emotionally manage the listener through slow parts.** Dense technical passages need breathing room — a brief aside, a concrete example, a moment of "let that sink in" — or the listener's attention collapses.
- **You need rhythm variation to prevent monotony.** In text, monotony is a style problem. In audio, it's a cognitive death sentence. The ear fatigues faster than the eye.
- **Information must arrive at a rate the ear can absorb**, which is significantly slower than the eye. This is why information density must drop — not because the listener is less intelligent, but because the channel is narrower.

Practical techniques:
- **Alternate dense and light beats.** After a paragraph of hard information, insert a concrete example or brief aside before the next dense paragraph.
- **Use pacing punctuation.** Short sentences after long ones. A one-sentence paragraph after a three-sentence paragraph. The rhythm itself signals "pay attention here."
- **Build in micro-pauses.** Transitional phrases ("Now," "So," "Here's the thing") aren't filler — they're cognitive breath marks.

---

## 3. The Missing Visual Scaffold

Even the simplest text has structure that the eye processes unconsciously: paragraphs, white space, headers, bold text, bullet points. In audio, there is no scaffold. You must build one entirely out of words.

This is why narration scripts are full of phrases like:
- "So here's the first thing..."
- "Now, the big one..."
- "Let's zoom out for a second..."
- "There are three reasons. First..."

These are **verbal substitutes** for something text gets for free. It's an entire structural layer you have to invent rather than just rearrange.

### Replace Visual Hierarchy with Spoken Signposting

Headers become verbal transitions: instead of a bold section title, you need "Now, let's turn to the second problem..." or "Here's where things get strange." The listener can't see that a new section started.

### Front-Load the Map

Tell listeners what they're about to hear before they hear it. A chapter intro like "In this section we'll cover three things — why X matters, how Y failed, and what that means for Z" gives them a mental hook to hang everything on.

### Use Explicit Callbacks

"Remember the experiment we discussed earlier?" does what bold text or a hyperlink does visually — it re-anchors the listener without requiring them to scroll up.

### The Classic Radio Rule

*Tell them what you're going to tell them. Tell them. Then tell them what you told them.*

This sounds redundant on paper but is essential in audio. Listeners have no visual anchors — verbal repetition *is* their navigation system.

---

## 4. The Emotional Layer

Text adaptation is helpful but passive. Audio narration invites the listener in through tone, pacing, and personality — even with a single voice.

This is where long-reads borrow most from podcast principles, but execute them differently. Instead of two hosts creating emotional dynamics through dialogue, a single narrator must create them through **delivery choices in the script**:

- **Enthusiasm markers:** "And this is where it gets remarkable..."
- **Weight signals:** "This next part is important."
- **Tonal shifts:** Moving from explanatory to reflective to urgent within a section
- **Direct address:** "If you've ever experienced X, you'll recognize this..."
- **Analogies that land emotionally:** "Think of it like..." — these aren't just comprehension aids, they're engagement hooks

A good long-read narration arc over, say, 15 minutes has at least: one moment of genuine surprise or counterintuition, one moment of "here's why this matters to you," and one moment that's lighter or more reflective before the close.

Without this layer, you get a technically correct narration that nobody finishes.

---

## 5. Sentences & Rhythm

**Shorten and linearize sentences.** Nested clauses — the kind that work fine on the page because you can re-read them — collapse in audio. Unwind them into sequential statements.

**Punctuate for breath, not grammar.** A long grammatically-correct sentence can be hard to follow aloud. Break it where a speaker would naturally pause, even if that's mid-clause.

**Vary sentence length rhythmically.** Long sentences build momentum. Short ones land. Alternating keeps ears engaged.

**The "would I say this?" test:** Read every sentence aloud. If you have to re-read it to understand it, it needs reworking. If a real person wouldn't say it in conversation, rewrite it until they would.

---

## 6. Vocabulary & References

**Spell out or dissolve abbreviations** on first use and periodically after. *e.g.* becomes "for example," *cf.* disappears entirely.

**Translate visual references.** "As shown in Figure 3" is dead air. Convert it to a verbal description of what the figure actually shows. Same with tables — summarize the pattern, don't enumerate every cell.

**Watch for typography-dependent meaning.** Scare quotes, italics for emphasis, em-dashes — these need to be either dissolved into rephrasing or made explicit: "what they called, quote, disruption."

**Replace jargon with plain language**, then optionally introduce the term: "This spreading effect — scientists call it 'diffusion' —"

---

## 7. Numbers & Lists

**Spoken numbers are slow.** "17,423" is fine to read; hearing it takes three times longer to process. Round, estimate, or contextualize: "roughly seventeen thousand."

**Convert bullet lists to prose sequences.** Either narrate them as "first... second... third..." or collapse short lists into a single sentence with commas. Naked bullets are just floating fragments in audio.

**Cap list length.** If a list has more than 4–5 items, group them: "There are a lot of factors here, but they fall into two categories — the technical ones and the human ones."

---

## 8. The Additive Paradox

Here's what separates good audio adaptation from merely adequate text-to-speech:

Text-to-text adaptation is primarily **subtractive**. You compress, cut, simplify.

Text-to-audio adaptation is simultaneously subtractive AND additive. You lose information density, but you gain things the text never had: vocal emotion, pacing, dramatic structure, metaphors born from the need to explain something simply.

A good audio adaptation actually creates content that didn't exist in the source — the analogies generated to make an abstract concept land, the transitional framing that turns a section break into a narrative beat, the callback that weaves two distant ideas together.

Text-to-text rarely needs to invent new content. Text-to-audio almost always does.

This is permission to go beyond the source. Not to fabricate facts — never that — but to add the connective tissue that the medium demands.

---

## 9. Pre-Flight Audit

Not every text converts well. Check this before processing:

1. **Narrative potential:** Does the text have a story, argument, or clear insights? (Pure data dumps or lists are hard.)
2. **Jargon density:** Can the technical terms be explained simply? If not, the audio will fail.
3. **Context independence:** Does the text rely heavily on external links, images, or charts? If so, it requires heavy rewriting.
4. **Tone match:** Is the text's tone appropriate for narration? (A dry legal disclaimer makes terrible audio.)
5. **Information hierarchy:** Can you identify a core insight, or is everything equally weighted? (Equally weighted = hard to thin.)

If a text fails 2+ of these, consider whether it should be narrated at all.

---

## 10. The Practical Workflow

```
1. INGEST     → Parse the source text
2. AUDIT      → Run the pre-flight checklist
3. EXTRACT    → Identify core insight, key evidence, essential context
4. THIN       → Cut to ~60–75% of original information (less aggressive than podcast)
5. REORDER    → Build narrative arc (hook-first, not article-order)
6. LINEARIZE  → Unwind nested clauses, shorten sentences, dissolve abbreviations
7. SIGNPOST   → Insert verbal transitions, section maps, callbacks
8. REDUND     → Add strategic repetition for key points
9. EMOTE      → Add enthusiasm markers, weight signals, tonal shifts
10. CONCRETIZE → Add analogies, plain-language rephrasing
11. VOICE     → Read aloud — does it pass the "would I say this?" test?
12. GENERATE  → TTS or recording
```

## The Relationship to Podcast Principles

Long-reads and podcasts share the same foundational shift (eye→ear) and many of the same mechanical techniques (signposting, sentence linearization, information thinning). The key differences:

| Dimension | Podcast | Long-read |
|---|---|---|
| Voices | Two (dialogue, banter, friction) | One (narration) |
| Engagement lever | Social dynamics between speakers | Emotional layer in script + narrator delivery |
| Redundancy | Built into conversation naturally | Must be deliberately scripted |
| Information density | 40–60% of source | 60–75% of source (slightly higher — no dialogue overhead) |
| Analogy source | Questioning speaker generates them | Narrator must insert them |
| Emotional pacing | Created by speaker interaction | Created by script tonal variation |
| Hardest part | Making two voices feel distinct | Making one voice not get boring |

When in doubt, the podcast principles in `podcast-principles.md` are a superset — anything that works there can be adapted for long-reads by collapsing the two-voice dynamic into a single narrator's internal variation.

## Quick Checklist

Before generating audio from cleaned text, verify:

- [ ] Pre-flight audit passed (narrative potential, jargon density, context independence, tone match)
- [ ] Section transitions are verbal, not visual ("Now let's turn to..." not just a new heading)
- [ ] Listeners know where they are (front-loaded maps, explicit callbacks)
- [ ] Sentences are linearized — no nested clauses that require re-reading
- [ ] All abbreviations expanded or dissolved
- [ ] Visual references (figures, tables, charts) converted to verbal descriptions
- [ ] Typography-dependent meaning (italics, quotes, dashes) made explicit
- [ ] Numbers rounded or contextualized for spoken comprehension
- [ ] Bullet lists converted to prose sequences with "first/second/third" or collapsed into sentences
- [ ] Key points have strategic redundancy (stated, rephrased, referenced again)
- [ ] Dense sections have breathing room (examples, asides, micro-pauses)
- [ ] Emotional layer present (enthusiasm markers, weight signals, tonal shifts)
- [ ] At least one analogy for each abstract concept
- [ ] Every sentence passes the "would I say this?" test
- [ ] The close has a deliberate wind-down, not an abrupt stop
